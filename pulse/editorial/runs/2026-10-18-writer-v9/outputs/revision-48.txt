<p>The Trump administration has taken the next step toward deciding whether the American Bar Association’s law school accrediting arm should keep its federal recognition, a move that could affect how some states think about alternatives to ABA approval.</p><p>The Education Department staff recommended ending federal recognition for the ABA’s accrediting council, according to <a href="https://www.foxbusiness.com/politics/trump-administration-takes-steps-chip-away-american-bar-associations-power-accredit-law-schools">Fox Business</a>. The ABA has held that role since 1952, and the final decision now rests with Under Secretary of Education Nicholas Kent.</p><p>Fox News reported that losing federal recognition would not automatically end the ABA’s accreditation of law schools or change state bar-admission rules. But it could speed up state efforts to consider alternative paths to becoming a lawyer.</p><p>“I think the biggest thing is, everyone should know that the federal recognition of the ABA is just the first step,” Adam Biggs, general counsel of the University of Austin, told <a href="https://www.foxnews.com/politics/becoming-lawyer-cheaper-faster-trump-admin-shakeup">Fox News</a>. “If they get knocked off their perch, that's going to supercharge the conversations of these state supreme courts to create alternative pathways that are not just the ABA.”</p><p>The Education Department said its staff reviewed the ABA’s accreditation standards and found the organization “out of compliance” with federal accreditor regulations, according to Fox Business. The department said in August that it would not comment in detail because the process was still moving through review stages, including a September hearing before an advisory committee.</p><p>Melissa Hart, chair of the ABA’s Accreditation Council, said in an August statement to Fox News Digital that the council was complying with federal laws and regulations. She said the group looked forward to addressing misconceptions and clarifying the record at its upcoming hearing before the committee next month.</p><p>Fox Business reported that the Education Department’s staff recommendation followed a process outlined under federal law. Under the Higher Education Act, the education secretary can terminate recognition of an accrediting agency only after notice and an opportunity for a hearing, and accreditors are generally given up to 12 months to come back into compliance before recognition is ended.</p><p><a href="https://www.highereddive.com/news/education-department-takes-first-step-to-strip-aba-of-accreditation-authori/828656/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Higher Ed Dive</a> reported that the National Advisory Committee on Institutional Quality and Integrity reviewed the ABA’s accreditation council at a Sept. 23 meeting. The outlet also reported that the panel later voted 11-1 to say it could not reach a recommendation, leaving Kent with 90 days to make a final determination.</p><p>Fox News also reported that the ABA’s rules have drawn scrutiny from the Justice Department, which has threatened an antitrust investigation over restrictions on adjunct professors teaching core law school courses. In an Oct. 7 letter, Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward gave the ABA 15 days to revise or justify its rules, The Wall Street Journal reported, as relayed by Fox News.</p><p>The ABA’s accrediting council accredits nearly 200 law schools, according to <a href="https://www.highereddive.com/news/federal-advisory-panel-dodges-decision-on-abas-accreditation-arm/831329" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Higher Ed Dive</a>. A council representative told the outlet that only five of those institutions rely on the agency’s accreditation to access federal student aid, while just over a dozen freestanding law schools depend on it for that purpose.</p><p>Even if the council lost federal recognition, Higher Ed Dive reported that it could continue accrediting law schools so their graduates meet state licensing requirements. State supreme courts generally set those rules, which often require graduates to have an ABA-accredited degree to sit for the bar exam.</p><p>Fox News reported that the potential shift could create openings for schools that want to offer cheaper or shorter programs outside the ABA model. One example is a proposed law school at the University of Austin, where Biggs said the university is considering a two-year track or a combined undergraduate and legal studies model, with free tuition and no federal funding.</p><p>The distinction between federal recognition and state licensing means the administration can affect which accreditor gets federal backing, but it cannot on its own change every state’s bar rules.</p>
