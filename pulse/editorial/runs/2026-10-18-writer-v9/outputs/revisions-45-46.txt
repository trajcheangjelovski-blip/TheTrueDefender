The Education Department has recommended denying renewal of the American Bar Association’s federal recognition as a law school accreditor, and the final decision now rests with Under Secretary of Education Nicholas Kent, Fox Business reported and Fox News reported.

The ABA’s accrediting council has held federal recognition since 1952, Fox Business reported. A federal advisory panel deadlocked over whether to recommend that the council lose that status, according to Higher Ed Dive.

Losing federal recognition would not automatically end the ABA’s role in accrediting law schools or change state bar-admission rules, Fox News reported. State supreme courts generally set those rules, and most states require graduates to have attended an ABA-accredited law school to qualify for the bar exam, the outlet reported.

The practical effect for students would depend on the school. Fox Business reported that the ABA’s accrediting council accredits nearly 200 law schools, while Higher Ed Dive said it is the gatekeeper to federal student aid for only five freestanding institutions of legal education. Fox News said most law students would be unlikely to lose access to federal loans because many law schools are part of larger universities with their own institutional accreditors.

Melissa Hart, chair of the ABA’s Accreditation Council, said in a statement to Fox News Digital that the council is complying with federal laws and regulations. She said the group looked forward to addressing misconceptions and clarifying the record at its hearing before the National Advisory Committee on Institutional Quality and Integrity. Hart also said, “As a national accrediting body for American law schools, we remain focused on ensuring quality legal education that produces competent, ethical attorneys who are eligible for licensure.”

Higher Ed Dive reported that Education Department staff had urged ending federal recognition after finding the ABA’s standards out of compliance with multiple federal criteria. The outlet also reported that Under Secretary Kent will have 90 days to make a final determination on whether to deny the ABA council federal recognition.

Adam Biggs, general counsel of the University of Austin, told Fox News Digital that federal recognition is only the first step. He said the university is considering a two-year law program or a model that combines undergraduate and legal studies, with free tuition and no federal funding.

Fox News reported that state supreme courts generally control bar-admission rules, which means graduates of schools approved through alternative state systems could face different requirements depending on where they want to practice.

The Education Department’s process follows the Higher Education Act, which gives the secretary power to end recognition only after notice and an opportunity for a hearing. Accreditors are generally given up to 12 months to come back into compliance before recognition is terminated, Fox Business reported.

NOAA says El Niño is continuing to strengthen, while California officials are using a September emergency proclamation to speed up storm preparations across the state.

In its latest ENSO Diagnostic Discussion, the NOAA Climate Prediction Center said the chance of a strong-to-very strong El Niño through January-March 2027 remains greater than 83 percent. The agency said sea surface temperature anomalies exceeded plus 4.0 degrees Celsius in portions of the eastern equatorial Pacific last month, and that the ocean-atmosphere system reflected a strengthening El Niño.

Gov. Gavin Newsom’s office said the September 21 emergency proclamation was meant to help the state move faster to protect communities, stage resources and prepare for severe weather, including strong storms and coastal flooding. The order directs state agencies to lead flood-risk-reduction efforts, stage flood-fighting supplies such as sandbags and pumps, and pre-position other critical equipment.

The governor’s office also said the proclamation prepares the California National Guard for flood response, search-and-rescue, engineering and logistics missions if needed. State environmental and natural resources agencies are also directed to support local efforts to prepare for flooding, landslides and debris flows.

“We are not waiting for this season’s storms to be on our doorstep,” Cal OES Director Caroline Thomas Jacobs said in a statement released by the governor’s office. “Cal OES is leading California’s statewide efforts by bringing state and local partners together, conducting advance planning efforts, and identifying potential needs before impacts occur.”

The governor’s office said a study published in Science found El Niño events are now more than 36% stronger than before the industrial era, with that trend accelerating over the last 40 years as the planet warms. The office described the study as evidence that climate change can intensify El Niño.

The governor’s office said the emergency proclamation is meant to support early planning for impacts that can include heavy rain, flooding, landslides and coastal flooding. NOAA said stronger El Niño events raise the chances of impacts consistent with the pattern, although those effects are not guaranteed.

California officials have already pointed to coastal damage in Southern California as they prepare for more storm activity. According to the New York Post, Long Beach leaders on Friday outlined a recovery plan after powerful surf from Tropical Storm Rachel damaged homes and infrastructure on the Peninsula. The newspaper said seven homes remained red-tagged, down from eight, and that officials still did not know how many might have to be demolished.

The New York Post also quoted Mayor Rex Richardson as saying, “We’re now shifting into preparing for the next storm while we recover from the impacts of Rachel.”

The NOAA discussion says the next ENSO Diagnostics Discussion is scheduled for 12 November 2026.
